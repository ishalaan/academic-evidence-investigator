Research Report

Research Question

What are the main applications of large language models in education?

Summary

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly applied in education to address diverse tasks, with their integration spanning instructional support, assessment, and personalized learning. A key application is the generation of instructional content, where LLMs create adaptive materials tailored to learners' needs, reducing the burden on educators (Ramos and Casas, no date). This capability is further enhanced by their role in teaching support and planning, where they assist in curriculum design and lesson development, enabling more efficient pedagogical workflows (Ramos and Casas, no date). Additionally, LLMs are employed in grading and assessment, automating tasks such as question generation and rubric-based evaluation. However, studies highlight challenges in reliability, particularly with automated systems struggling against OCR errors and the complexity of language-specific nuances, such as in German language exams, where localized models failed to produce usable outputs (Basaran, 2026). These findings underscore the need for domain-specific adaptations and improved multimodal workflows to enhance accuracy (Basaran, 2026).

Beyond automation, LLMs are integrated into question-answering (QA) systems that combine knowledge graphs (KGs) with their language-processing capabilities. This hybrid approach allows for context-aware learning by retrieving and contextualizing information from structured educational resources, improving the precision of answers to complex queries (Kok Arslan, 2025). Such systems support personalized learning by aligning responses with learners' prior knowledge and educational goals, thereby fostering deeper engagement. The technical framework proposed in this work demonstrates how LLMs can be leveraged to create intelligent, context-sensitive tools that bridge gaps between structured knowledge and dynamic learning needs (Kok Arslan, 2025). These applications highlight the potential of LLMs to transform educational technology by making learning more interactive and responsive.

Despite these advancements, the adoption of LLMs in education faces persistent challenges. Misinformation risks, algorithmic bias, and equity of access remain critical concerns, as highlighted by studies emphasizing the need for transparent evaluation methods and ethical frameworks (Ramos and Casas, no date). For instance, automated grading systems, while efficient, often lack the nuanced understanding required to assess subjective elements of language learning, such as creativity or critical thinking (Basaran, 2026). This gap underscores the importance of human oversight and hybrid models that combine AI-driven efficiency with human judgment. Furthermore, the reliance on automated systems raises questions about over-dependence, particularly in settings where equitable access to technology is limited (Ramos and Casas, no date). These limitations suggest that while LLMs can augment educational practices, their integration must be carefully managed to avoid exacerbating existing inequalities.

Looking ahead, emerging trends such as multimodal integration and embodied avatars are poised to expand the role of LLMs in education. These innovations aim to create more immersive and interactive learning experiences, though their effectiveness requires further empirical validation (Ramos and Casas, no date). The study on German language exams also points to the necessity of refining OCR technologies and visual input methods to ensure reliable automated assessment (Basaran, 2026). As the field evolves, prioritizing methodological rigor in evaluating LLMs—particularly in diverse educational contexts—will be essential to realizing their full potential. By addressing current limitations and fostering interdisciplinary collaboration, educators and developers can harness LLMs to create more equitable, effective, and adaptive learning environments.

Findings

  1. Large language models (LLMs) are being integrated into educational QA systems through the combination of knowledge graphs (KGs) and LLMs, enabling context-aware question-answering that leverages structured knowledge for accurate, resource-driven responses (Kok Arslan, 2025). This approach enhances learning by aligning answers with high-quality educational materials, supporting both formative and summative assessments. The technical framework demonstrates how KGs refine LLM outputs, ensuring coherence with pedagogical concepts and reducing errors in complex subject areas.
  2. Automated assessment using LLMs faces significant reliability challenges, particularly in language-specific tasks, due to OCR errors and limitations in visual input processing. While LLMs like fine-tuned LLaMA models show promise in localized grading, human evaluators maintain higher accuracy and consistency, highlighting the need for improved multimodal workflows and explainable scoring mechanisms before AI can reliably replace manual grading (Basaran, 2026).
  3. Emerging applications of LLMs include multimodal integration and embodied avatars for interactive tutoring, which personalizes learning experiences by adapting to individual student needs and contextual cues. These innovations aim to bridge gaps in traditional pedagogical methods, though their effectiveness remains under investigation due to methodological gaps in evaluation frameworks (Ramos and Casas, no date).
  4. LLMs are increasingly used for tutoring and learning assistance, providing real-time feedback and adaptive resources that support self-paced learning. This application reduces educator workload while fostering autonomy, though concerns about over-reliance on automated systems and potential bias in generated content persist (Ramos and Casas, no date).
  5. The adoption of LLMs in education is accompanied by persistent challenges, including misinformation risks, equity of access, and academic integrity issues. Studies emphasize the need for domain-specific adaptation, robust bias mitigation strategies, and ethical guidelines to ensure responsible integration of LLMs into curricular and assessment practices (Ramos and Casas, no date).

Limitations

  • The review identifies methodological gaps in evaluating LLMs across diverse educational contexts, such as inconsistent metrics for assessing bias or equity (Ramos and Casas, no date). Further evidence on standardized evaluation frameworks would clarify how to measure fairness and accessibility in different settings.
  • Automated grading systems face technical limitations, particularly with OCR errors and language-specific complexities like German’s case system, which hinder reliable assessment (Basaran, 2026). Improved multimodal workflows and domain-specific training could address these challenges, but more studies are needed on cross-linguistic adaptability.
  • Integrating knowledge graphs with LLMs for educational QA systems remains underexplored, with no mention of scalability or real-world implementation hurdles in the abstract (Kok Arslan, 2025). Evidence on long-term usability and alignment with curricular standards would strengthen claims about their practical impact.

    Read abstract

    This work presents a review of recent literature on the use of Large Language Models (LLMs) in education, with the goal of clarifying their current applications, methodological foundations, and emerging directions. The analysis is guided by research questions addressing where LLMs are being applied, what strategies are used to implement them, how they are evaluated, and what challenges accompany their adoption. To answer these questions, we focused on recent studies published in diverse educational settings and synthesized evidence across multiple domains. The findings show that LLMs are being applied in five major areas: instructional content generation, teaching support and planning, grading and assessment, question and quiz generation, and tutoring and learning assistance. Across these domains, LLMs enable adaptive materials, automate pedagogical tasks, and support personalized learning, yet they also introduce persistent challenges related to misinformation, bias, academic integrity, equity of access, and over-reliance on automated systems. Beyond mapping current practices, the review identifies methodological gaps in evaluation, recurring limitations across domains, and emerging trends such as multimodal integration, embodied avatars, multi-agent orchestration, and personalization. Drawing from these insights, this work provides both a critical assessment of the state of the field and a forward-looking perspective intended to guide future research and practice in the responsible integration of LLMs into education.

    Cited in Overview

    Read abstract

    This study investigates the use of large language models (LLMs)—ChatGPT-5, Claude Opus 4.1, Gemini Advanced 2.5 Pro, DeepSeek Pro, Qwen-3 Max, and Mistral Le Chat Pro—and a locally fine-tuned LLaMA 3.3 70B Instruct model for automating assessment tasks in language education. Specifically, the study looks to examine LLM capabilities in automating assessments with authentic midterm exam sheets from a “German as a Foreign Language” (GFL) course in three different scenarios: (1) general-purpose LLM with pre-corrected samples giving a score, (2) localized grading using a fine-tuned LLaMA model and reference answer keys, and (3) manual grading with and without a visual overlay technique. Human grading, supported by a structured scoring process, remained nearly perfect in terms of accuracy and reliability, whereas the local model failed because OCR and visual input techniques did not produce usable outputs. These findings reinforce the necessity for domain-specific adaptation, the design of stronger OCR and multimodal workflows, and explainable scoring mechanisms before local AI solutions can reliably contribute to the assessment of language learning tasks in applied educational settings.

  7. Language, Simulation, Imagination Large Language Models as Social TechnologyRelevance score: 12

    Paper ID: 0fe326633d40baac5986

    Abstract-only evidence

    Francesco Zorgno · No date · Crossref

    Below the relevance filter

    Read abstract

    Large language models (LLMs) have reopened fundamental questions in the philosophy of language and in the ethics of artificial intelligence. Conversational systems such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and comparable models now produce fluent, context-sensitive discourse without possessing consciousness, intentionality or semantic understanding in the human sense. Their relevance cannot therefore be captured either by treating them as artificial minds or by dismissing them as merely deceptive linguistic devices. This working paper interprets LLMs as social technologies of communication. Drawing on Daniel Dor's account of language as a technology for the instruction of imagination, it argues that the philosophical significance of LLMs lies less in whether they 'really understand' and more in the way they transform the conditions under which meaning is generated, attributed and validated. The paper situates this claim between innatist and functionalist accounts of language, emphasizing both the limits of anthropomorphism and the practical effectiveness of artificial linguistic performance. The paper also distinguishes between pure deception, in which users mistakenly attribute human-like understanding to the system, and conscious deception, in which the fiction of dialogue is knowingly accepted as a pragmatic interface. The conclusion proposes a relational account of intelligence: LLMs are not intelligent subjects, but human-LLM interactions may participate in intelligent processes when they generate meaningful, adaptive and critically controlled outcomes.

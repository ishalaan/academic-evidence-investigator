Research Question

How does generative AI affect student learning outcomes in higher education?

Summary

Generative AI (GenAI) in higher education shows mixed but promising effects on student learning outcomes, with its impact mediated by factors like student engagement and AI literacy. Studies highlight its potential to enhance academic tasks such as writing, research, and problem-solving, though effectiveness varies depending on contextual and individual factors. For instance, GenAI tools support personalised learning through adaptive feedback systems, which can improve engagement and reduce faculty workload (D, 2026). However, the same tools may also risk over-reliance, particularly in disciplines where critical thinking is paramount (Hon, 2025). The mediating role of student engagement and AI literacy is central: higher engagement and stronger AI literacy correlate with better learning outcomes, suggesting that GenAI’s benefits are amplified when students actively interact with the technology and understand its limitations (Alkhatib et al., 2026). This aligns with findings that GenAI’s utility depends on institutional frameworks that integrate it with pedagogical goals, ensuring alignment with learning objectives rather than treating it as a standalone solution (Riaz and Mushtaq, 2024).

Systematic reviews reveal conflicting evidence on GenAI’s overall impact. While some studies report increased engagement and performance, others note limitations, such as disciplinary variability in effectiveness and concerns about academic integrity (Hon, 2025). For example, in Nigerian higher education, GenAI combined with learning analytics showed potential for personalised learning but raised ethical concerns about plagiarism and the erosion of critical skills (Elenode, 2026). These findings underscore the need for balanced integration, where GenAI complements rather than replaces traditional teaching methods. Student perceptions further highlight this duality: while many recognise GenAI’s benefits for brainstorming, research, and writing support, they also express worries about accuracy, privacy, and the long-term impact on personal development (Chan and Hu, 2023). This suggests that successful implementation requires addressing both technical and ethical challenges, alongside fostering digital literacy among students.

The evidence also emphasises the importance of institutional preparedness. A framework for GenAI integration highlights the interplay between technological adoption, pedagogical strategies, and student characteristics, advocating for tailored approaches that consider institutional resources and student needs (Riaz and Mushtaq, 2024). For instance, qualitative studies stress the need for faculty training to ensure GenAI is used ethically and effectively, rather than as a crutch for administrative tasks (McKenzie and Day, 2025). Additionally, the role of AI literacy as a mediator indicates that educational institutions must prioritize teaching students how to critically engage with AI tools, distinguishing between assistance and dependency (Alkhatib et al., 2026). This aligns with calls for curricula that blend GenAI with critical thinking exercises, ensuring students can evaluate AI-generated content and apply it meaningfully to their work (Shuvo et al., 2025).

Practically, the synthesis suggests that GenAI’s impact on learning outcomes hinges on intentional design and contextual adaptation. While it can enhance specific academic functions like writing and research, its broader educational value depends on how it is integrated into pedagogical practices. Institutions should focus on creating frameworks that balance innovation with ethical considerations, such as transparency in AI use and safeguards against misuse. Furthermore, fostering student agency—through training in AI literacy and critical engagement—will be key to maximising GenAI’s potential without compromising academic integrity. These insights highlight the need for ongoing research to refine GenAI’s role in higher education, ensuring it supports, rather than undermines, the development of well-rounded, critically aware learners.

  • The mediating role of AI literacy suggests variability in GenAI's effectiveness across student populations, necessitating further research on targeted literacy programs (Alkhatib et al., 2026).
  • Conflicting evidence on engagement and performance highlights the need for longitudinal studies to assess long-term impacts and disciplinary differences (Hon, 2025).
  • Ethical concerns, such as academic integrity risks, require deeper exploration of institutional safeguards and pedagogical integration strategies (Elenode, 2026).
  • Over-reliance on GenAI for tasks like writing may undermine critical thinking, warranting studies on balanced tool usage frameworks (Chan and Hu, 2023).
  • Institutional preparedness gaps, such as technological infrastructure and faculty training, demand evidence on scalable implementation models (Riaz and Mushtaq, 2024).

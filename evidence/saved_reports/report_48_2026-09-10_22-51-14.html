Research Question

What are the main uses of XGBOOST?

Summary

XGBoost is primarily employed for predictive analytics across diverse domains, with a focus on time series forecasting, classification tasks, and handling imbalanced datasets. In healthcare, it has been applied to predict daily confirmed COVID-19 cases in Europe, leveraging its ability to optimise parameters for robust model fitting (Gopatoti, 2023). Similarly, in the telecommunications sector, XGBoost is fine-tuned to address data imbalance and overfitting challenges, enhancing accuracy in predicting customer churn through precision, recall, and accuracy metrics (P a and R B, no date). These applications highlight its adaptability to complex, real-world data scenarios where traditional methods may falter. The algorithm’s strength lies in its gradient boosting framework, which iteratively corrects errors and improves predictive performance, making it suitable for both regression and classification tasks. Its integration with feature engineering techniques, such as adjusting parameter features in pandemic prediction models (Gopatoti, 2023), further underscores its flexibility in tailoring solutions to specific problems.

Beyond these domains, XGBoost has been explored for medical diagnostics, such as detecting Parkinson’s disease through machine learning models. While the abstract for this application is truncated, the use of XGBoost in this context suggests its potential for identifying subtle patterns in clinical data (malhotra, no date). This aligns with its broader utility in scenarios requiring high-dimensional data analysis, where its ability to handle non-linear relationships and interactions between variables is advantageous. However, the absence of detailed performance metrics in the abstract limits the depth of evaluation for such applications. The algorithm’s capacity to manage imbalanced datasets, as demonstrated in customer churn prediction (P a and R B, no date), also extends to other fields, such as fraud detection or rare event forecasting, where skewed data distributions are common.

A key advantage of XGBoost is its interpretability, particularly when combined with tools like SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) for explaining model predictions. While this is explicitly mentioned in the context of corporate ESG rating prediction (Zhang and Zhao, 2026), the absence of a full abstract prevents a detailed analysis of its implementation. Nevertheless, the algorithm’s integration with optimisation techniques—such as grid search for hyperparameter tuning in hybrid models like LSTM-XGBoost (Liu, 2025)—demonstrates its compatibility with advanced methodologies. These hybrid approaches often aim to leverage the strengths of multiple models, with XGBoost providing robust predictive capabilities. However, the lack of abstracts for many sources, including those involving deep learning or composite models (Islam et al., 2022), restricts the ability to assess its performance in these contexts.

Practically, XGBoost’s efficiency and scalability make it a popular choice for industries requiring rapid, accurate predictions. Its ability to handle large datasets and parallel processing capabilities further enhance its applicability in resource-constrained environments. However, the reliance on feature engineering and hyperparameter tuning, as noted in pandemic prediction studies (Gopatoti, 2023), highlights the need for domain-specific expertise to maximise its effectiveness. While the algorithm’s performance in medical diagnostics (malhotra, no date) and customer churn prediction (P a and R B, no date) is promising, the absence of comprehensive validation in these areas underscores the importance of rigorous testing. Overall, XGBoost’s versatility and performance in diverse applications position it as a valuable tool, though its implementation requires careful consideration of data characteristics and model-specific requirements.

Findings

  1. XGBoost is widely used for time series forecasting, exemplified by its application in predicting daily COVID-19 cases in Europe, where it outperformed other models using RMSE and MAPE metrics (Gopatoti, 2023). Its ability to optimise parameter features for robust predictive modelling makes it suitable for complex, real-world datasets (Gopatoti, 2023). In healthcare, it has been applied to detect Parkinson’s Disease, leveraging its classification capabilities to analyse neurological data (malhotra, no date). The algorithm’s gradient boosting framework enables iterative error correction, enhancing accuracy in both regression and classification tasks. In telecommunications, XGBoost is fine-tuned to address data imbalance and overfitting, improving customer churn prediction through precision, recall, and accuracy (P a and R B, no date). These applications highlight its adaptability to diverse domains, from public health to business analytics, where traditional methods may struggle with data complexity (P a and R B, no date).

Limitations

  • The reliance on RMSE and MAPE for evaluating XGBoost models in S1 limits generalizability, as these metrics may not capture all aspects of predictive accuracy. Further evidence on alternative evaluation methods or real-world validation would strengthen claims about its applicability beyond controlled datasets (Gopatoti, 2023).
  • Fine-tuning XGBoost for imbalanced datasets in S2 depends on the effectiveness of introduced feature functions, which are not explicitly validated against alternative approaches. Comparative testing with other imbalance mitigation techniques would clarify its relative advantages (P a and R B, no date).
  • S3’s abstract truncates details on dataset size, validation methods, or cross-domain applicability, restricting assessment of XGBoost’s robustness in Parkinson’s disease detection. Full methodology reports would address gaps in understanding its scalability to other medical conditions (malhotra, no date).

Research Question

How does generative AI affect student learning outcomes in higher education?

Summary

Generative AI (GenAI) demonstrates multifaceted impacts on student learning outcomes in higher education, with evidence highlighting both supportive applications and mediating factors. Studies indicate that GenAI tools enhance academic tasks such as writing, research, and problem-solving by providing instant feedback and organizational support (Alkhatib et al., 2026). For instance, GenAI assists in structuring ideas and creating outlines, which can improve the clarity of academic work (Alkhatib et al., 2026). However, the effectiveness of these tools hinges on students’ engagement and AI literacy, which act as critical mediators between GenAI usage and learning outcomes (Khanal, 2025). Research underscores that students who actively engage with GenAI and possess higher AI literacy are more likely to achieve better academic results, suggesting a need for targeted interventions to foster these competencies (Alkhatib et al., 2026).

The integration of GenAI into assessment systems also shows promise, with studies reporting that AI-driven feedback mechanisms can personalise learning and reduce faculty workload (D, 2026). These systems offer real-time evaluations, enabling students to identify gaps in understanding more efficiently. However, concerns about the reliability of AI-generated feedback and its potential to reduce critical thinking remain underexplored, highlighting a gap in empirical validation (D, 2026). Similarly, GenAI’s role in personalised learning is supported by evidence linking its adoption to improved student engagement and academic performance, particularly when aligned with pedagogical strategies (Elenode, 2026). Mobile learning platforms powered by AI further enhance accessibility, allowing students to engage with content flexibly, though their long-term efficacy in diverse educational contexts requires further scrutiny (Mabrouk, 2026).

While GenAI offers opportunities for innovation, challenges such as ethical awareness and equitable access persist. Some studies emphasise the need for frameworks that address AI literacy and ethical governance to ensure responsible use (Shuvo et al., 2025). For example, GenAI’s potential to streamline tasks may inadvertently reduce opportunities for deep learning, necessitating balanced integration with traditional methods (Shuvo et al., 2025). Additionally, the creative application of GenAI in fields like arts and interdisciplinary projects is gaining traction, though its pedagogical impact on educational equity remains under-researched (Rong et al., 2025). These findings suggest that while GenAI can augment learning, its success depends on contextual factors such as institutional support, student preparedness, and ethical considerations.

Practically, the evidence points to several implications for higher education. Institutions should prioritize training programmes to enhance AI literacy and foster critical engagement with GenAI tools (Khanal, 2025). Integrating GenAI into assessment systems requires careful design to maintain academic rigour and prevent over-reliance on automated feedback (D, 2026). Furthermore, hybrid approaches combining AI-driven support with human oversight may optimise learning outcomes, ensuring that technology complements rather than replaces pedagogical expertise (Elenode, 2026). As GenAI continues to evolve, ongoing research into its long-term effects on student engagement, equity, and ethical practices will be essential to guide its implementation effectively (Shuvo et al., 2025).

Findings

  1. A quantitative study involving undergraduate and postgraduate students found that Generative AI's impact on learning outcomes is mediated by student engagement and AI literacy, with higher engagement and literacy correlating with improved academic performance (Alkhatib et al., 2026).
  2. A systematic review of AI-driven mobile learning platforms highlighted their potential to enhance student engagement and academic performance, though long-term efficacy across diverse contexts remains underexplored (Mabrouk, 2026).
  3. A qualitative study analysing GenAI integration emphasised the need for pedagogical alignment and institutional preparedness, with frameworks linking technological acceptance, engagement, and ethical governance (Riaz and Mushtaq, 2024).
  4. Research on AI-based assessment systems reported reduced faculty workload and personalised feedback as benefits, though concerns about feedback reliability and critical thinking reduction were noted (D, 2026).
  5. A study with 197 postgraduate students identified AI literacy components (technical proficiency, critical evaluation, ethical awareness) as key predictors of academic performance, mediated by student engagement (Khanal, 2025).

Limitations

  • The reliance on AI-generated feedback lacks empirical validation for its reliability and impact on critical thinking, necessitating further studies on accuracy and long-term effects (D, 2026).
  • Ethical governance frameworks for GenAI remain underdeveloped, requiring more research on integrating AI literacy and ethical awareness into educational policies (Shuvo et al., 2025).
  • The long-term efficacy of AI-driven tools in diverse educational contexts is unclear, demanding longitudinal studies to assess sustained impacts on learning outcomes (Mabrouk, 2026).
  • Structural barriers to equitable AI integration, such as access disparities, are underexplored, warranting analysis of institutional and socioeconomic factors (Rong et al., 2025).
  • The mediating roles of student engagement and AI literacy require deeper exploration to refine strategies for maximising GenAI’s educational benefits (Alkhatib et al., 2026).

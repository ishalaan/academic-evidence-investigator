Research Report

Research Question

What are the main applications of large language models in education?

Summary

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly applied in education to enhance personalised learning, automate assessments, and support pedagogical innovation. Their use spans content creation, language learning, and task-specific support, with studies highlighting both opportunities and challenges. For instance, LLMs like ChatGPT are employed as learning aids and research assistants, aiding students in problem-solving, literature reviews, and foreign language acquisition (Čávojský et al., 2023). Similarly, tools such as ZeroGPT are developed to detect AI-generated content, addressing concerns about academic integrity and cognitive offloading (Čávojský et al., 2023). These applications demonstrate LLMs’ potential to streamline educational workflows while raising ethical questions about reliance on AI for critical thinking (Čávojský et al., 2023).

A key area of application is assessment automation, where LLMs are used to generate grading criteria, provide targeted feedback, and evaluate student work across multiple languages. However, performance in non-English languages remains uneven, correlating with the volume of training data available for those languages (Gupta et al., 2025). This highlights the need for careful implementation to avoid exacerbating educational inequalities. In Chinese education, frameworks like EduEval are introduced to rigorously evaluate LLMs through hierarchical benchmarks that align with cognitive learning objectives, ensuring alignment with educational standards (Ma et al., 2025). Such tools aim to balance innovation with accountability, addressing risks associated with untested deployments (Ma et al., 2025).

In specialised fields like programming education, LLMs are integrated into learning processes, though their impact on student outcomes is nuanced. While they can assist with code generation and debugging, over-reliance on LLMs for tasks requiring critical thinking—such as algorithm design—correlates with reduced learning gains (Jošt, Taneski and Karakatič, 2024). This underscores the importance of balancing AI support with human oversight to preserve essential skills. Similarly, in sports sciences, LLMs are used to create context-specific teaching resources and translate academic content into accessible formats, though careful validation is required to ensure accuracy and relevance (Boukari et al., 2026). These examples illustrate the dual role of LLMs as both enablers and potential disruptors of traditional pedagogical practices.

Beyond technical applications, LLMs are being explored for their capacity to address systemic issues in education. Frameworks mapping the LLM life cycle identify biases in data curation, model training, and deployment, which could perpetuate existing inequalities if left unaddressed (Lee et al., 2024). Additionally, educators are called to adopt hybrid models that integrate LLMs for administrative tasks while preserving face-to-face instruction, kinesthetic learning, and mentorship—key components of embodied education (Boukari et al., 2026). Such approaches emphasise the need for pedagogical frameworks that align AI tools with educational goals rather than replacing human expertise. Overall, the evidence suggests that LLMs’ educational applications are most effective when paired with intentional design, ethical considerations, and ongoing evaluation (Lee et al., 2024).

Findings

  1. Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as learning aids and research assistants, supporting tasks like problem-solving, literature reviews, and foreign language acquisition. ChatGPT, for instance, is highlighted as a tool to enhance student engagement while raising concerns about cognitive offloading and academic integrity. Tools like ZeroGPT are also developed to detect AI-generated content, addressing ethical dilemmas in education (Čávojský et al., 2023).
  2. LLMs facilitate the creation of teaching resources and translation of academic content into accessible formats, aiding educators in contextualizing findings for practitioners. This application bridges the research-practice gap in sports pedagogy by translating complex concepts into plain language summaries, though it requires careful human oversight to ensure accuracy (Boukari et al., 2026).
  3. Personalised learning and assessment automation are key applications, with LLMs enabling tailored educational experiences. The EduEval benchmark, designed for Chinese K-12 education, evaluates LLMs across cognitive dimensions like memorization, reasoning, and ethics, ensuring alignment with educational standards (Ma et al., 2025).
  4. Multilingual performance disparities in LLMs pose challenges for non-English language education. While models perform reasonably well in major languages, lower-resource languages like Ukrainian and Telugu show poorer task accuracy, highlighting the need for more inclusive training data to avoid exacerbating educational inequalities (Gupta et al., 2025).
  5. In programming education, LLMs like ChatGPT and Copilot are integrated into software development courses, but their overuse correlates with reduced critical thinking skills in tasks like code generation. This suggests a need for balanced implementation to preserve foundational learning outcomes (Jošt, Taneski and Karakatič, 2024).
  6. LLMs are employed to automate assessments and provide targeted feedback, though their deployment risks undermining traditional pedagogical practices. Studies emphasise the importance of hybrid models that combine AI-driven efficiency with human mentorship to maintain embodied learning and social interaction in education (Boukari et al., 2026).
  7. Ethical and algorithmic bias concerns are central to LLM integration in education. Frameworks mapping the LLM life cycle identify biases in data curation, model training, and customization, urging systematic mitigation to prevent reinforcing existing educational disparities (Lee et al., 2024).

Limitations

  • The multilingual capabilities of LLMs are constrained, with poorer performance in low-resource languages due to limited training data, raising concerns about equitable access to educational tools (Gupta et al., 2025). Further evidence on cross-linguistic task performance and resource allocation could clarify scalability. Algorithmic bias in LLMs may perpetuate educational inequalities, as seen in grading and feedback tasks, though the study notes this requires deeper analysis of training data sources (Lee et al., 2024). More granular research on bias mitigation strategies in diverse educational contexts is needed. Over-reliance on LLMs for tasks like coding or problem-solving correlates with reduced critical thinking skills, highlighting risks of cognitive offloading (Jošt, Taneski and Karakatič, 2024). Longitudinal studies tracking skill development alongside AI integration would strengthen understanding of these trade-offs. Ethical concerns around academic integrity and AI-generated content remain underexplored, with existing tools like ZeroGPT only addressing surface-level issues (Čávojský et al., 2023). Systematic investigations into pedagogical frameworks balancing AI use and human oversight are critical.

    Objective: The global burden of physical inactivity contributes to 5.3 million deaths annually, exceeding smoking-related mortality in certain regions and contributing substantially to the 1.5 billion individuals worldwide living with chronic diseases. In this work, we ascertain if their use in education settings in non-English languages is warranted. We evaluated the performance of popular LLMs on four educational tasks: identifying student misconceptions, providing targeted feedback, interactive tutoring, and grading translations in eight languages (Mandarin, Hindi, Arabic, German, Farsi, Telugu, Ukrainian, Czech) in addition to English.     Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly adopted in educational contexts to provide personalized support to students and teachers. The unprecedented capacity of LLM‐based applications to understand and generate natural language can potentially improve instructional effectiveness and learning outcomes, but the integration of LLMs in education technology has renewed concerns over algorithmic bias, which may exacerbate educational inequalities. Building on prior work that mapped the traditional machine learning life cycle, we provide a framework of the LLM life cycle from the initial development of LLMs to customizing pre‐trained models for various applications in educational settings. We explain each step in the LLM life cycle and identify potential sources of bias that may arise in the context of education. We discuss why current measures of bias from traditional machine learning fail to transfer to LLM‐generated text (eg, tutoring conversations) because text encodings are high‐dimensional, there can be multiple correct responses, and tailoring responses may be pedagogically desirable rather than unfair. What is already known about this topic The life cycle of traditional machine learning (ML) applications which focus on predicting labels is well understood. Biases are known to enter in traditional ML applications at various points in the life cycle, and methods to measure and mitigate these biases have been developed and tested. Large language models (LLMs) and other forms of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) are increasingly adopted in education technologies (EdTech), but current evaluation approaches are not specific to the domain of education. What this paper adds A holistic perspective of the LLM life cycle with domain‐specific examples in education to highlight opportunities and challenges for incorporating natural language understanding (NLU) and natural language generation (NLG) into EdTech. Potential sources of bias are identified in each step of the LLM life cycle and discussed in the context of education. A framework for understanding where to expect potential harms of LLMs for students, teachers, and other users of GenAI technology in education, which can guide approaches to bias measurement and mitigation. Implications for practice and/or policy Education practitioners and policymakers should be aware that biases can originate from a multitude of steps in the LLM life cycle, and the life cycle perspective offers them a heuristic for asking technology developers to explain each step to assess the risk of bias. Measuring the biases of systems that use LLMs in education is more complex than with traditional ML, in large part because the evaluation of natural language generation is highly context‐dependent (eg, what counts as good feedback on an assignment varies). EdTech developers can play an important role in collecting and curating datasets for the evaluation and benchmarking of LLM applications moving forward.

Since LLMs are a relatively new phenomenon, little is known about the factors influencing their acceptance in learning design; therefore, this research is needed, as learning design principles are the bedrock of student engagement and success. Methodology: A cross-sectional correlational quantitative study was employed. Data was collected using an online questionnaire posted on social media, including LinkedIn, from 203 instructional designers and university teaching academics. Purposive and snowball sampling methods were used to target instructional designers and university teaching academics at colleges and universities worldwide. Participants were asked to share the survey link with fellow instructional designers and university-teaching academics in their communities. Confirmatory factor analysis was used to verify the fit of the data on the measurement model. First-order and second-order structural modelling were used to identify the structural relationships among the variables. Using LLMs will free up more time for teaching academics and instructional designers so that they spend more time on higher-order thinking skill demands. Consequently, the students will be exposed to more engaging and interactive content, resulting in improved learning outcomes.

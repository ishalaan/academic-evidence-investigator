Research Report

Research Question

How does generative AI affect student learning outcomes in higher education?

Summary

Generative AI has shown potential to enhance student learning outcomes in higher education, though its impact varies depending on implementation and context. Studies indicate that AI-driven assessment systems can improve conceptual understanding, error correction, and student satisfaction through real-time feedback (D, 2026). For instance, a mixed-method study found that AI-based tools significantly boosted academic performance and self-regulated learning, though concerns about academic integrity and algorithmic bias persist (D, 2026). Similarly, research on AI-assisted writing tools revealed a strong positive correlation between students’ perceptions of these tools and their academic writing performance, with ethical use further predicting better outcomes (Ishtiaq, Naseem and Akbar, 2026). These findings suggest that when integrated thoughtfully, generative AI can support skill development and engagement, but its effectiveness hinges on alignment with pedagogical goals.

However, the evidence also highlights challenges and risks associated with AI adoption. Over-reliance on AI tools has been linked to diminished critical thinking skills and ethical concerns, such as the erosion of academic integrity (Limosinero, 2025). A literature review noted variability in AI’s impact, with some studies reporting minimal gains or negative effects in specific contexts, underscoring the importance of how tools are integrated into curricula (Almaqtari, Alramada and Alsabry, 2026). For example, while AI can enhance efficiency in tasks like writing and research, its misuse—such as cheating or superficial learning—requires proactive governance. The need for clear policies, AI literacy training, and ethical frameworks is emphasised across multiple studies, reflecting a consensus on the necessity of balancing innovation with responsibility (Limosinero, 2025).

The role of human expertise remains critical in maximising AI’s benefits. Research suggests that generative AI should complement, rather than replace, educators’ guidance. For instance, a study on AI’s use in literature reviews and data analysis highlighted the importance of combining AI’s efficiency with human oversight to ensure quality and depth (Almaqtari, Alramada and Alsabry, 2026). Similarly, the integration of AI into teaching practices must address students’ and staff’s attitudes and knowledge, as strategic adoption can enhance engagement and wellbeing (McKenzie and Day, 2025). This aligns with findings that AI tools are most effective when paired with training and clear guidelines, ensuring they support rather than undermine educational objectives.

Despite these insights, the evidence is not without limitations. Many studies rely on self-reported data or small samples, which may affect the generalisability of results (Almaqtari, Alramada and Alsabry, 2026). Additionally, the long-term effects of AI on deeper learning outcomes, such as critical thinking or creativity, remain underexplored. While some studies suggest AI can improve efficiency and engagement, others caution against over-reliance, highlighting the need for further research on sustainable integration strategies. Overall, the evidence supports a nuanced view: generative AI holds promise for enhancing learning outcomes when used ethically and strategically, but its success depends on addressing systemic challenges and fostering a culture of responsible innovation.

Research on Grade 9 students highlighted positive experiences with AI tools, including improved topic understanding and assignment efficiency, but also raised concerns about critical thinking erosion and academic integrity, alongside calls for AI literacy training and policy frameworks (Limosinero, 2025). Limitations

  • The evidence highlights variability in AI's impact, with some studies reporting minimal gains or negative effects in specific contexts, underscoring the need for curriculum-aligned integration (Almaqtari, Alramada and Alsabry, 2026).
  • Over-reliance on AI tools risks diminishing critical thinking skills and ethical awareness, necessitating clearer guidelines and training to mitigate misuse (Tamayo et al., 2025; Limosinero, 2025).
  • Concerns about academic integrity and algorithmic bias persist, requiring further research on detection mechanisms and equitable AI design (D, 2026; Limosinero, 2025).

    The rapid advancement of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI) has significantly transformed assessment and feedback mechanisms in higher education. Traditional assessment practices often suffer from delayed feedback, limited personalization, and high faculty workload, which may negatively affect student engagement and learning outcomes. Generative AI–based assessment and feedback systems offer adaptive, real-time, and personalized evaluation models that promise enhanced learning effectiveness. This study evaluates the impact of Generative AI–driven assessment and feedback systems on student learning outcomes in higher education institutions. Using a mixed-method research design, data were collected from undergraduate and postgraduate students exposed to AI-supported assessments across multiple disciplines. Statistical tools such as simple percentage analysis, paired t-tests, and regression analysis were employed to assess improvements in academic performance, learner engagement, and self-regulated learning. The findings reveal that Generative AI–based feedback significantly improves conceptual understanding, timely error correction, and student satisfaction. However, concerns regarding academic integrity, algorithmic bias, and ethical data use remain critical challenges. The study concludes that while Generative AI can substantially enhance assessment effectiveness, its successful adoption requires ethical governance, faculty training, and transparent AI models. The paper offers practical implications for educators, policymakers, and institutional leaders aiming to integrate AI responsibly into higher education assessment frameworks.

    Artificial intelligence (AI) is reshaping academic practice across research and learning. To address fragmented evidence, this review aims to (i) map commonly used academic AI tools, (ii) synthesize reported effects on research and learning performance, and (iii) outline risks and governance needs. We conducted a qualitative content analysis using publicly available “materials,” including official tool documentation and websites, major academic databases , and user reports; records were screened for relevance to higher education and research, data were charted on features, use-cases, and ethical considerations, and tools were categorized into five domains: AI writing assistants; literature-review & citation tools; data analysis & visualization; productivity & organization; and learning & tutoring.     Paper ID: 2702a498b04650f790be

    Mushba Ishtiaq, Dr. Afshan Naseem, Gulnaz Akbar · 2026 · Crossref

    Cited in Overview

    Read abstract

    This study explores how students' perceptions of AI-supported learning tools are related to their academic writing outcomes, and how responsible and ethical use of AI can directly predict these outcomes. The research design used was quantitative correlational research design. A sample of 204 undergraduate and postgraduate students from public and private universities in Pakistan was selected for collecting data using a structured questionnaire which was rated on a five-point Likert scale. The instrument assessed three constructs: AI-Assisted Writing Perceptions (AIWPS), Academic Writing Performance (AWPS), and Ethical and Responsible AI Writing Use (ERAIWUS). Pearson correlation and multiple regression of statistical analysis were performed. The findings showed that students' perceptions of the AI-assisted writing tools were positively correlated with the academic writing performance (r = .800, p < .001). There was also a strong positive correlation between ethical and responsible use of AI and academic writing performance (r = .503, p < .001). Multiple regression analysis was performed to determine if both variables explained a significant portion of the variance in academic writing performance. Both variables were found to be significant and together accounted for 64.9% of the academic writing performance variance (R² = .649, F(2, 201) = 185.82, p < .001). The study findings suggest that AI-powered learning tools can be successfully used to support students' academic writing abilities in an ethical and responsible way. The results underscore the need to foster responsible use of AI in the educational context to enhance the potential value of AI technologies and preserve academic integrity.

    This study explores the use and impact of AI tools on Grade 9 students’ learning, focusing on their experiences, challenges, and ethical concerns. Through a combination of survey questionnaires and interviews, data were collected from Grade 9 students to assess the extent to which AI tools are utilized for academic tasks and their perceived effectiveness. The results revealed that most students have positive experiences using AI tools, citing improved understanding of class topics and increased efficiency in completing assignments. However, concerns about the potential erosion of critical thinking skills and maintaining academic integrity were raised, aligning with research by Cornell University and Turnitin. The study also uncovered challenges such as over-reliance on AI tools and a lack of understanding of ethical practices, underscoring the importance of fostering responsible AI use. Based on these findings, recommendations were made to establish clear policies, incorporate AI literacy into the curriculum, and provide teacher training on AI integration. This study contributes valuable insights into the role of AI tools in modern education and offers practical strategies for promoting their responsible use in academic settings.

Research Question

How does generative AI affect student learning outcomes in higher education?

Summary

Generative AI has demonstrated both potential benefits and challenges for student learning outcomes in higher education. Studies indicate that AI-driven assessment systems can enhance conceptual understanding, error correction, and student satisfaction through real-time, personalised feedback (D, 2026). For example, mixed-method research found statistically significant improvements in academic performance and self-regulated learning when students used AI-supported assessments, though concerns about academic integrity and algorithmic bias persist. These findings highlight the dual role of generative AI as a tool for educational enhancement and a source of ethical complexity. However, the effectiveness of such systems depends on factors like faculty training and transparent AI design, which are critical for mitigating risks (D, 2026).

A separate body of evidence focuses on the limitations of AI detection tools, which are often used to address concerns about academic dishonesty. While platforms like Turnitin and GPTZero are widely adopted, systematic reviews reveal their susceptibility to false positives, particularly when detecting content from multilingual or non-native English writers (Akram, 2026). This raises questions about the fairness and reliability of these tools, as their accuracy varies with text type and writing conditions. The study underscores that automated detection systems alone cannot resolve integrity issues, emphasising the need for complementary pedagogical strategies and ethical frameworks to address the root causes of academic misconduct.

The perception of AI-generated content also plays a pivotal role in shaping its educational impact. Research on social media environments shows that explicit labels significantly reduce perceived authenticity of AI-generated images, with a 0.27 standard deviation decrease in authenticity ratings (Pawelczyk, Dimmery and Yan, no datea). This 'implied authenticity effect' suggests that labeling strategies influence not only direct interactions with AI content but also broader attitudes toward its use. However, the study also notes that factors like internet literacy and age moderate these effects, indicating that the societal and individual contexts of AI adoption are complex. These findings align with calls for transparent AI labeling, particularly in educational settings where trust in digital tools is paramount (Pawelczyk, Dimmery and Yan, no datea).

Beyond technical and ethical considerations, generative AI is increasingly framed as a means to enhance pedagogical practices and student engagement. A symposium on AI in higher education highlights strategic opportunities to integrate AI into teaching, such as using it to improve student wellbeing, motivation, and employability (McKenzie and Day, 2025). For instance, a curriculum redesign at a health science program embedded generative AI in assessments to build educator and student capabilities, positioning AI as a collaborative tool rather than a replacement for human expertise. Such approaches suggest that AI’s value lies in its ability to augment learning experiences, provided it is implemented with clear ethical guidelines and aligned with institutional goals.

The evidence collectively suggests that generative AI’s impact on learning outcomes is mediated by its design, implementation, and the broader educational context. While AI can enhance assessment efficiency and personalisation, its integration requires careful governance to address integrity risks and ensure equitable access. The studies also highlight the importance of fostering digital literacy and critical engagement with AI tools, as students and educators navigate the evolving landscape of technology-enhanced learning. Future research should explore longitudinal effects of AI adoption and its role in diverse educational settings, particularly in regions with varying regulatory frameworks and cultural attitudes toward technology.

Findings

  1. A mixed-method study involving undergraduate and postgraduate students found statistically significant improvements in academic performance, conceptual understanding, and self-regulated learning when using AI-supported assessments, though concerns about academic integrity and algorithmic bias were noted (D, 2026).
  2. A systematic review of AI detection tools revealed variability in their effectiveness, with platforms like Turnitin and GPTZero showing susceptibility to false positives, particularly for multilingual or non-native English content, highlighting reliability and fairness challenges (Akram, 2026).
  3. A survey experiment with 877 participants demonstrated that explicit labels on AI-generated content reduced perceived authenticity by 0.27 standard deviations, while an 'implied authenticity effect' showed labeled content slightly increased perceived authenticity in unlabeled images, influenced by factors like internet literacy and age (Pawelczyk, Dimmery and Yan, no datea).

Limitations

  • The studies primarily focus on short-term outcomes, lacking longitudinal data on sustained learning impacts (D, 2026). Further evidence on long-term academic trajectories and skill retention is needed to assess generative AI's enduring effects. AI detection tools' effectiveness varies across text types and writing conditions (Akram, 2026), but few studies explore their performance with non-native English writers or multilingual contexts. More research is required to evaluate fairness and equity in diverse educational settings. Perceived authenticity of AI-generated content depends on user characteristics like age and digital literacy (Pawelczyk, Dimmery and Yan, no datea), yet most studies assume homogeneous user groups. Future research should investigate how demographic and cultural factors moderate these effects. Few studies address faculty training and institutional support as critical factors for ethical AI integration (D, 2026). Evidence on how professional development influences AI adoption and student outcomes remains underexplored. The ethical governance of AI systems is discussed but rarely quantified (D, 2026). Further analysis of institutional policies and their alignment with pedagogical goals would clarify practical implementation challenges.

    Arslan Akram · 2026 · Crossref

    Read abstract

    The rapid expansion of generative artificial intelligence and large language models has dramatically altered the creation of written material. While these technologies can enhance the learning process, but, as well, they have created significant concerns regarding academic integrity, authorship and the fair assessment of students. In response, numerous institutions have already implemented AI-detection software that aims at determining whether some text was authored by a human or generated by AI systems. But there are concerns about the validity, correctness and equity of such tools in the actual fields of education. This paper gives a systematic review of empirical studies concerning AI text detection tools in learning institutions. In order to identify literature published between 2022 and 2026, it was thoroughly searched in large academic databases such as Scopus, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect, following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Through the use of rigorous inclusion/exclusion criteria, a total of 17 empirical studies were selected for in-depth analysis. In the review, the researchers examine the different types of research methodologies used to evaluate the performance of AI detection tools, the accuracy of these tools in identifying AI-generated content, and the effectiveness, fairness, and usability of these tools. The results show that the tools, including Turnitin, GPTZero, Copyleaks, ZeroGPT, and Originality.ai, are frequently used, but their effectiveness varies depending on the type of text and writing conditions. It is often mentioned in the literature that such systems become quite easily affected by paraphrasing, translation or writing style, and that they can falsely categorize the texts written by multilingual or nonnative writers of English. The review also presents a number of technical, ethical and pedagogical difficulties with the continuation of the use of automated detection systems only. Upon the evidence synthesized, the study elucidates the importance of careful and open usage of AI detection technologies in learning and suggests the directions of future research to enhance its consistency and at the same time encourages ethical academic procedures.

    Paper ID: f2f9d4687e8658ad2f92

    Fabian Pawelczyk, Drew Dimmery, Pu Yan · No date · Crossref

    Cited in Overview

    Read abstract

    This study investigates how labeling AI-generated content (AIGC) influences users’ perceptions of authenticity in social media environments. Motivated by emerging global regulations mandating the disclosure of AI-generated media, we designed a pre-registered survey experiment to test two main questions: (1) Do AI labels reduce the perceived authenticity of AI-generated images? (2) Does exposure to labeled content affect perceived authenticity in unlabeled images (a potential spillover effect)? The pre-analysis plan and materials are available on OSF (see link below). We conducted a survey experiment with a German sample (N = 877) in which participants were randomly assigned to one of three groups: a control group without labels, a process-based label group (“AI-generated”), or a harm-based label group (“Misleading”). Participants viewed twelve Instagram-style posts, six of which contained AI-generated or AI-altered content. Perceived authenticity was measured by asking whether the depicted events actually occurred. Our results show that both labeling strategies significantly reduced perceived authenticity of AI-generated images, with average reductions of about 0.27 standard deviations. We also find evidence of an implied authenticity effect: exposure to labeled content slightly increased perceived authenticity in unlabeled images (about one-fifth the size of the direct labeling effect). Exploratory analyses indicate that internet skills and age are associated with perceived authenticity differences. By embedding labels in Instagram-style posts, our study increases ecological validity compared to earlier work focused on headlines or generic stimuli. Situated in Germany, a frontrunner in digital platform regulation, the findings provide rare non-U.S. evidence on AI labeling and contribute directly to ongoing policy debates.

    The rapid integration of artificial intelligence (AI) has transformed digital marketing by enabling brands to create personalized, automated, and data-driven promotional content. This study examines the impact of AI-generated marketing content on consumer behavior, with a specific focus on purchase intention. The study investigates how consumer perception, perceived credibility, perceived authenticity, and consumer trust influence consumers' willingness to purchase products promoted through AI-generated marketing content. A quantitative research design was adopted, and primary data were collected through a structured online questionnaire using a five-point Likert scale. Out of 184 initial responses, 154 valid responses were retained after data screening. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Cronbach's alpha reliability testing, Pearson correlation, and multiple regression analysis, supplemented by Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) diagnostics to assess multicollinearity and Harman's single-factor test to evaluate common method bias. The reliability results showed excellent internal consistency across all constructs (Cronbach's alpha > 0.95). These findings suggest that AI-generated marketing content can significantly influence consumer purchase intention—particularly among young, digitally active consumers—when it is perceived as authentic, trustworthy, and aligned with consumer expectations. The study contributes to AI marketing literature by highlighting the mediating role of trust in the credibility–purchase intention pathway and underscores the centrality of authenticity in consumer acceptance of AI-driven marketing communication.

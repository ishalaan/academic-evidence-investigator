Research Question

How does generative AI affect student learning outcomes in higher education?

Summary

Generative AI in higher education shows mixed but promising effects on student learning outcomes, with evidence highlighting both opportunities and challenges. Studies suggest that AI-driven assessment systems can provide adaptive, real-time feedback, potentially enhancing learning effectiveness by addressing individual student needs (D, 2026). Similarly, tools like Generative AI are linked to improved academic performance when paired with high student engagement and AI literacy, which act as mediating factors in determining outcomes (Alkhatib et al., 2026). These findings align with broader observations that AI integration can boost engagement through personalised content and interactive learning opportunities, though its success depends on how effectively institutions support its implementation (Riaz and Mushtaq, 2024). However, the evidence is not uniformly positive. Some studies report mixed results, noting that over-reliance on AI may undermine critical thinking, while effectiveness varies across disciplines and institutional contexts (Hon, 2025). For instance, in Pakistani higher education, students acknowledge AI’s utility in saving time but also express concerns about academic integrity and the need for ethical frameworks (Majeed et al., 2025). These tensions underscore the importance of balancing AI’s benefits with safeguards against misuse.

A critical theme is the role of institutional and pedagogical alignment in maximizing AI’s impact. Research emphasises that successful integration requires not only technological readiness but also faculty training and clear guidelines for ethical use (Elenode, 2026). For example, studies in Nigerian higher education highlight that personalised learning through AI and learning analytics improves engagement and academic performance, but only when supported by robust digital literacy and academic integrity measures (Elenode, 2026). Similarly, the use of Generative AI in creative fields, such as AI art, demonstrates potential to foster interdisciplinary projects and multimodal expression, though barriers like faculty training and unclear evaluation standards hinder equitable access (Rong et al., 2025). These examples illustrate that AI’s effectiveness is contingent on contextual factors, including institutional preparedness and pedagogical design.

The evidence also reveals gaps in understanding long-term impacts and disciplinary differences. While some studies report positive correlations between AI adoption and learning outcomes, others caution against premature conclusions due to limited longitudinal data and variability in implementation (Hon, 2025). For instance, a systematic review found conflicting results, with some studies highlighting increased engagement and performance while others noted limitations such as over-reliance on AI or inconsistent effectiveness across fields (Hon, 2025). This variability suggests that the impact of Generative AI may not be universal and could depend on factors like student demographics, institutional resources, and the specific applications of AI. Additionally, ethical concerns, such as academic integrity and the potential for bias in AI-generated content, remain underexplored in many studies (Shuvo et al., 2025).

Practically, the evidence points to several implications for higher education. Institutions should prioritize training for both students and faculty to ensure responsible AI use and integrate ethical frameworks into AI adoption strategies (Majeed et al., 2025). Pedagogical approaches must align with AI capabilities, leveraging tools for personalised feedback while fostering critical thinking and digital literacy (Alkhatib et al., 2026). Furthermore, longitudinal studies are needed to assess long-term effects and address disciplinary disparities in AI effectiveness (Hon, 2025). By combining technological innovation with thoughtful implementation, higher education can harness Generative AI to enhance learning outcomes while mitigating risks. These steps would ensure that AI serves as a supplementary tool rather than a replacement for human-driven educational practices.

Findings

  1. Generative AI-driven assessment systems provide adaptive, real-time feedback, which can enhance learning effectiveness by addressing individual student needs through personalised evaluation models (D, 2026). This aligns with findings that such systems reduce delays in feedback and faculty workload, potentially improving engagement and academic performance (Das, Chaudhery and Das, 2025). However, their success depends on institutional integration and faculty training to ensure alignment with pedagogical goals (Riaz and Mushtaq, 2024).
  2. Student engagement and AI literacy act as critical mediating factors in determining the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes. Studies show that students who actively engage with AI tools and possess foundational knowledge of their responsible use achieve better academic results, suggesting a need for targeted literacy programs (Alkhatib et al., 2026). This is corroborated by evidence that AI adoption correlates with improved teaching effectiveness and learner satisfaction when paired with structured training (Das, Chaudhery and Das, 2025).
  3. Mixed evidence highlights both opportunities and challenges: while some studies report increased engagement and performance through AI-generated content and interactive learning opportunities, others caution against over-reliance on AI, noting variable effectiveness across disciplines and risks of reduced critical thinking (Hon, 2025). This underscores the importance of balanced implementation strategies (Elenode, 2026).
  4. Students in Pakistani higher education report using generative AI primarily to save time on academic tasks, though they acknowledge limitations such as potential plagiarism risks and ethical concerns (Majeed et al., 2025). This reflects a broader trend where AI is perceived as a supplementary tool rather than a replacement for traditional learning, with its impact mediated by institutional policies on academic integrity (Shuvo et al., 2025).
  5. Generative AI art applications in higher education support creative ideation and interdisciplinary projects, though barriers like limited faculty training and unclear evaluation standards hinder equitable access (Rong et al., 2025). This suggests that pedagogical integration of AI requires addressing structural challenges to maximize its potential for diverse learning outcomes (Riaz and Mushtaq, 2024).

Limitations

  • The reliance on self-reported data in some studies, such as S2 and S3, may introduce bias, as student engagement and AI literacy are subjective constructs. Further evidence from objective metrics or longitudinal tracking could strengthen validity (Alkhatib et al., 2026).
  • Mixed-method studies like S1 and S6 often lack detailed reporting on control groups or confounding variables, limiting causal inferences. More rigorous experimental designs with comparative cohorts would clarify AI's direct impact (D, 2026).
  • Findings from region-specific studies, such as S5 (India) and S10 (Pakistan), may not generalize to global higher education contexts. Cross-cultural comparative research is needed to assess universal applicability (Majeed et al., 2025).
  • The absence of standardised evaluation frameworks in most studies (e.g., S4, S9) complicates benchmarking AI's pedagogical effectiveness. Unified metrics for measuring learning outcomes would enhance comparability (Riaz and Mushtaq, 2024).
  • Short-term studies, such as S8's 2018–2024 review, lack longitudinal data on sustained impacts. Longitudinal analyses could reveal long-term effects on academic performance and institutional adaptation (Hon, 2025).
